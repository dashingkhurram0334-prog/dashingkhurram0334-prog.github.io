Biometric

NADRA Iris Scan Service

NADRA has been quietly rolling out a third layer of biometric identification across its registration centers — iris scanning. If you've noticed a small camera-and-screen device sitting next to the fingerprint scanner at your local NADRA office and wondered what it does, here's a plain-language look at what iris capture is, why NADRA added it, and who actually needs to use it.

From Two Biometric Markers to Three

For years, NADRA verified identity using two biometric markers: fingerprints and facial recognition. Fingerprints were the original standard, and facial matching was added later as a backup — useful for the small number of people whose fingerprints are faint, worn down from manual labor, or otherwise difficult for a scanner to read.

Iris scanning is the third marker in this system. It captures the unique pattern in the colored part of the eye, which — unlike fingerprints — stays essentially unchanged for most of a person's life. NADRA introduced this capability after amendments to the NADRA Rules 2002 formally recognized iris capture as part of the legal definition of biometric data, and it has since expanded to counters across a growing number of centers nationwide, with tens of millions of iris records already captured.

Why Iris Scanning Matters Most for Children

The clearest case for iris scanning is child registration. Children under 18 make up nearly half of Pakistan's registered population, and their physical features change rapidly. A three-year-old's fingerprints are not yet fully formed and are unreliable for matching. A face photographed at age three looks very different by age nine or eleven, as the child's features develop.

This creates a real identity-security gap: without a stable biometric marker, children's records were historically easier to duplicate or misuse, since there was little reliable way to confirm that a name and a face still belonged to the same child years later. Iris patterns, by contrast, are established by around age two and remain stable for decades, giving NADRA a dependable way to anchor a child's identity to their Child Registration Certificate (CRC) even as the rest of their appearance changes.

Because of this, iris capture is now built into the CRC application process. When a Child Registration Certificate is applied for, the device photographs the child and captures an iris scan as part of the same biometric enrollment — at no additional cost beyond the standard CRC fee. Iris capture is not currently mandatory for every application channel; applications submitted purely through the Pak Identity app, for example, do not include an iris scan, while applications processed at a physical NADRA center with the sensor present do.

Self-Service Kiosk Machines at NADRA Centers

Alongside the iris sensors, many centers now have ATM-style self-service kiosks. These allow a citizen whose biometric details haven't changed — no update to their name, address, or family particulars — to walk through a straightforward identity-renewal process on their own: verifying fingerprints or face, choosing a delivery address, and generating a QR code used to pay the applicable fee, without needing a counter appointment. An on-site facilitator is typically available to guide first-time users through the screen.

These self-service machines are being expanded to more cities over time, aimed at reducing counter congestion — particularly in high-traffic Union Council areas — by giving routine renewal applicants a faster, independent path through the process.

Mobile Registration Tablets

NADRA has also deployed portable tablet devices, built domestically, that carry the same core software as the Pak Identity platform. These tablets can capture fingerprints, photographs, and iris scans in the field, letting registration teams travel to schools and Union Council offices to handle child registration and family-record updates — such as recording a birth, marriage, or divorce — without requiring families to travel to a fixed center.

A Brief Timeline of NADRA's Biometric System

NADRA's move to iris scanning did not happen overnight. It builds on more than two decades of gradually expanding biometric capability across the organization's registration network.

Stage What Changed
Early biometric era Identity verification relied on fingerprint capture alone, recorded at the counter during application.
Facial recognition added A photograph-based facial matching layer was introduced as a fallback for applicants whose fingerprints were difficult to read.
NADRA Rules amended (2025) Government-approved amendments to the NADRA Rules 2002 formally added iris capture to the legal definition of biometric data.
Iris sensors installed Dedicated iris-capture devices began appearing at counters in a growing number of cities, alongside the existing fingerprint and camera equipment.
Self-service and mobile expansion ATM-style kiosks and field tablets extended biometric capture beyond the traditional counter, reaching Union Councils and schools directly.

Why This Matters Beyond Just Convenience

It's easy to read all of this as simply another layer of paperwork, but the underlying motivation is identity protection. Pakistan's registered population includes tens of millions of children under 18, and until recently, this group had the weakest biometric anchor of any age bracket — fingerprints not yet formed, and faces that change year to year. That gap made child identity records comparatively easier to duplicate or misrepresent than an adult's.

Iris capture closes that specific gap. It doesn't replace the photograph or the fingerprint — both are still recorded — but it adds a marker that stays reliable long after a child's appearance has changed completely, giving families and the state a more durable way to confirm that a registered identity still belongs to the same person years later.

How the Three Biometric Markers Compare

Each of NADRA's three biometric markers plays a different role in the verification process. Understanding how they differ helps explain why the system now relies on all three together rather than any single one.

Marker When It's Reliable Main Limitation
Fingerprints From roughly age 12 onward, once ridge patterns are fully formed Can wear down from manual labor, burns, or age; unreliable for young children
Facial Recognition Useful as an immediate backup when fingerprints fail to scan Appearance changes significantly with age, especially in children
Iris Scan Stable from approximately age two through old age Requires a dedicated sensor, which is not yet installed at every counter

Taken together, the three markers give NADRA overlapping ways to confirm identity, so that if one method is unavailable or produces an unclear reading, another can still be used without stopping the application process.

Step-by-Step: What Happens During an Iris Scan

For applicants going through a counter where the iris sensor is installed, the process is quick and does not require any special preparation. In general terms, it follows this sequence:

  1. Standard photograph. The operator first takes the regular identity photograph used on the card itself, exactly as in a normal application.
  2. Fingerprint capture. Fingerprints are recorded as usual, using the standard scanner at the counter.
  3. Iris capture. The applicant looks briefly into a small camera-and-light unit mounted near the counter screen. The device captures the pattern of both eyes in a few seconds; no touching or contact with the device is involved.
  4. Address and delivery details. The applicant confirms where the finished card or certificate should be sent or picked up from.
  5. Payment. A QR code is generated for the total fee, which already includes any biometric enrollment — there is no separate charge shown for the iris step.

The entire additional step for the iris scan typically adds only a few seconds to the overall application process, since it does not require ink, pressure, or repeated attempts the way a poor fingerprint reading sometimes does.

What This Means for Families Applying for a CRC

For parents applying for a Child Registration Certificate, the practical takeaway is simple: if you apply in person at a center equipped with the iris sensor, your child's photograph and iris pattern will be captured together as part of the same visit, alongside the usual family documents. There is nothing extra to prepare or bring — the process is built into the standard CRC counter workflow.

Families applying through the Pak Identity app rather than visiting a center in person should note that the iris step is tied to the physical sensor hardware, so app-only applications proceed without it. If having your child's iris pattern on record matters to you specifically, applying at a physical center rather than through the app is the way to ensure that step is included.

Data Security Considerations

Because iris data is a sensitive biometric identifier, it is captured and stored using the same infrastructure and legal framework that already governs fingerprint and facial data under the NADRA Ordinance and the 2025 amendments to the NADRA Rules. It is not shared publicly and is used strictly for identity verification purposes tied to a citizen's or child's official record.

As with any biometric system, the goal of adding a third marker is to make it harder — not easier — for someone to fraudulently obtain a card under another person's identity, since matching all three markers together is significantly more difficult to falsify than matching just one.

Is There an Extra Fee for Iris Scanning?

No. Iris capture is included within the standard fee for the identity document being processed — whether that's a CNIC or a Child Registration Certificate. NADRA does not charge a separate line-item fee for the iris scan itself.

What Documents Should You Bring If Iris Capture Applies to You

The iris scan itself does not require any extra documentation beyond what's already needed for the underlying application. If you're applying for a Child Registration Certificate at a center with the iris sensor installed, the standard CRC document checklist still applies:

  • Original CNIC of both parents (or guardian, where applicable)
  • Original hospital birth certificate or Union Council-issued birth certificate
  • Parents' original marriage documentation, where requested
  • The child's original CRC, if this is a renewal rather than a first-time application

The operator handles the photograph, fingerprint, and iris steps as part of the same counter visit — there is nothing separate to bring or fill out for the biometric portion itself.

A Note on Rollout Pace

Because the iris sensors and self-service kiosks are being installed gradually rather than all at once, availability still varies by city and even by specific center. If your local NADRA office doesn't yet have the iris device, your application is processed exactly as before, using fingerprint and facial matching — you won't be turned away or asked to travel elsewhere solely because the iris hardware isn't present at that location.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do adults need to get an iris scan too?
Adults are not required to undergo a separate iris enrollment. The feature is primarily built around strengthening child identity records, though the underlying biometric system now supports all three markers — fingerprint, facial, and iris — for future verification needs.

What happens if the iris scanner isn't available at my center?
Not every NADRA center or counter currently has the iris sensor installed. If it isn't present, your application proceeds using the standard fingerprint and facial biometric process; the iris scan is an additional layer where available, not a blocking requirement.

Can I use the self-service kiosk for a new CNIC application?
Self-service kiosks are intended for straightforward renewals where no personal details have changed. Applications involving corrections, first-time registration, or changes to family particulars still need to go through a standard counter.

Does my child's iris scan need to be repeated as they grow older?
No. Once an iris pattern is established at around age two, it does not need to be recaptured as the child grows, unlike a photograph, which is typically updated with each renewal as the child's appearance changes.

Is the iris scan painful or uncomfortable for a young child?
No. The device does not touch the eye at all — it uses a camera and a brief light from a short distance, similar to having a photograph taken. Most children are captured within a few seconds without difficulty.

Will iris scanning eventually become mandatory for everyone?
NADRA has not announced a timeline for making iris capture mandatory across all applicant types. For now, it remains primarily built into the child registration workflow at centers equipped with the sensor, with broader rollout continuing city by city.
